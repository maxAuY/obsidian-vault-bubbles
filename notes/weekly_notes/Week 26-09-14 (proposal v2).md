# Background

### Applications

>[!NOTE] Multidisciplinary applications
>- Cavitation rheology has applications across several fields such as biology, chemistry, materials, and mechanics.
>- It can be used to characterise both fluids and solids.
>- In medicine, it is often used to quantitatively characterise disease progression by monitoring tissue damage and tissue properties.

>[!NOTE] Characterising fibrin density
>- Fibrin density is correlated with several pathological processes e.g. fibrosis (scarring of the liver)
>- Thrombus stiffness can be estimated by fibrin density
>- Characterising fibrin density can therefore be used to understand tissue health and monitor disease progression.

>[!NOTE] Characterising physical properties
>- Physical properties of soft tissues are not known for strain rates in the MHz range at which microbubbles are driven.
>- The elasticity, shear modulus, and viscosity can be measured using rheology which has a wide range of industrial and biomedical applications.
>- Detecting changes in In Vivo properties (characterise resolution and sensitivity). 
>- Measuring rheology of soft tissues at high strain rates for modelling microbubbles for imaging and therapy.

### Techniques

>[!NOTE] Rheometry
> - There are several standard techniques for characterising cavitation at lower strain rates including parallel plate, pulse-echo, and atomic force microscopy (AFM).
> - Higher strain rate rheology (>10 kHz) is performed using Kolsky Bar rheology which is not suited for heterogeneous, low-wave speed biomaterials.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[sreedasyam_2026_laser_cavitation_rheology.pdf#page=2&selection=52,18,57,34&color=yellow|sreedasyam_2026_laser_cavitation_rheology, p.2]]
> > The Split-Hopkinson (Kolsky) pressure bar remains the gold standard for high strain rate mechanical testing, but its reliance on stress wave propagation makes it poorly suited for spatially heterogeneous, low-wave speed biomaterials

![[sreedasyam_2026_laser_cavitation_rheology.pdf#page=2&rect=147,89,457,344&width=400|sreedasyam_2026_laser_cavitation_rheology, p.2]]

>[!NOTE] Cavitation rheology
> - There are several techniques that use cavitation to perform rheometry.
> - These span a range of strain rates, shear moduli, and size scales which makes the techniques flexible and widely applicable.
> - These techniques are applicable In Vivo to soft materials and biological tissues where standard techniques often fail.
> - Techniques include needle induced cavitation, laser induced cavitation, acoustic induced cavitation, and less common techniques such as shockwave-induced and confinement-induced cavitation.
> - Cavitation rheology most frequently uses high speed imaging to capture the bubble dynamics to characterise the material.
> - High speed imaging is not practical for widespread adoption in biomaterials and mechanobiology laboratories.
> - A new imaging technique needs to be developed for opaque materials.

>[!NOTE] Needle induced cavitation
>- A needle is used to puncture the sample and pressure is applied to create a single bubble.
>- The pressure required to cause material failure can be linked to the material properties.
>- This method works across a broad range of size scales.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[barney_2020_cavitation_in_soft_matter.pdf#page=4&selection=5,11,7,62&color=yellow|barney_2020_cavitation_in_soft_matter, p.4]]

>[!NOTE] Laser induced cavitation
>- Focused optical energy is used to induce cavitation in a material.
>- The cavitation is often monitored in real time using high-frame rate cameras.
>- Estrada et al. characterised material properties by comparing the cavitation dynamics to theoretical models.
>- Sreedasyam characterised material properties by mapping oscillation time to material elasticity. They measured oscillation time using a separate laser probe.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[barney_2020_cavitation_in_soft_matter.pdf#page=4&selection=213,57,216,61&color=yellow|barney_2020_cavitation_in_soft_matter, p.4]]
> > Due to the typical timescale of the cavitation dynamics, which ranges from milliseconds to microseconds, the real-time observation of LIC process is usually achieved by high-frame rate cameras (39).

>[!NOTE] Inertial cavitation
>- Several methods monitor inertial cavitation dynamics to perform microrheology.
>- They use lasers to initiate inertial cavitation and compare the bubble dynamics (either radius vs time or acoustic emissions) to simulated bubble dynamics with known material parameters.

- Estrada 2018 was the first to use inertial cavitation for microrheology, noting that the parameters $G$ and $\mu$ were responsible for noting that different material properties were responsible for different sections of the response of a collapsing bubble.
- They ran simulations, fitting first the shear modulus then the viscosity to the measured radius vs time data.

![[estrada_2018_high_strain_rate_soft_material_characterisation_inertial_cavitation.pdf#page=3&rect=33,138,512,455&color=yellow&width=400|estrada_2018_high_strain_rate_soft_material_characterisation_inertial_cavitation, p.293]]

- Mancia 2021 measures inertial collapse time (time between maximum radius and inertial collapse) and connects it to the Young's modulus of the material.
- They record bubble radius vs time and run simulations while changing the material parameters $G$, $\alpha$, and $\mu$ and the initial bubble radius $R_0$ to fit the data.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[mancia_2021_acoustic_cavitation_rheometry.pdf#page=4&selection=2,39,36,6&color=yellow|mancia_2021_acoustic_cavitation_rheometry, p.2934]]
> > For each experimental radius vs. time data set, we perform a series of simulations iterating over a maximum of four uncertain material parameters (G, a, m, R0 ) using one of three assumed models
> 
> 

- Yang 2024 measured inertial collapse time using hydrophones by calculating the time difference between the shockwaves created at bubble initiation and collapse. 
- They fit this data to a modified Keller-Miksis model to extract $G$ and $\mu$.

# Novelty

- Acoustic cavitation using ultrasound contrast agents is relatively unexplored in the field of rheology.
- This method is capable of taking rheological measurements at high strain rates through opaque tissues, both of which are not achievable using traditional methods.
- Previous studies have not used spectral features to estimate material properties.
